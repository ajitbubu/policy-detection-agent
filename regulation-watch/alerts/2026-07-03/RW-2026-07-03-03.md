───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
WHAT CHANGED (≤4 sentences, plain English)
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Chile is replacing its 1999-era data protection law (Ley 19.628, which had no real cookie/consent regime and no dedicated regulator) with a GDPR-aligned framework: a new independent regulator (APDP), additional lawful bases beyond consent (contract performance, legitimate interest), mandatory breach notification, full ARCO rights (access/rectification/cancellation/opposition, now aligned to erasure/portability/objection), and — per SERNAC-aligned guidance already circulating — an expected opt-in cookie-banner model with non-essential cookies disabled by default and a working reject/withdraw mechanism. The old regime had no functional cookie-consent requirement; the new regime is Latin America's most GDPR-like cookie/consent standard outside Brazil. This crossed the 180-day-to-effective threshold during this scan's lookback window (180 days before 2026-12-01 falls on 2026-06-04), which is the trigger for surfacing it now rather than waiting.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
WHO IS AFFECTED
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
- Applicability test: Any controller/processor processing personal data of individuals in Chile, regardless of where the controller is established (extraterritorial reach modeled on GDPR Art. 3).
- DataSafeguard customers likely affected: Any tenant with Chilean traffic that currently ships a US/LatAm-generic (or no) cookie banner in Chile. Estimate: broad — this is a net-new jurisdiction requiring an opt-in banner where today there is likely none or a low-friction one.
